01
FLUID BEHAVIOR

A fluid is a smooth field

Zoom in far enough and water is molecules. At engineering scales, we usually work with smooth averages.

PREDICT FIRSTIf I halve my mesh size, does water become less of a continuum?
The reasoning

No. Continuum validity compares molecular scales with physical flow scales. Mesh size controls how well your calculation resolves those fields.

Too small

A sample catches too few molecules. Its measured density jumps around.

Useful middle

Many molecules fit inside, but the sample still describes one local region.

Too large

The sample blends different parts of the flow. Real variation disappears.

ENGINEER’S HABIT

First choose a physical model. Then choose a mesh fine enough to resolve that model.

The equation, in plain language Optional depth
\[ \mathrm{Kn} = \frac{\lambda}{\ell} \]
\(\lambda\)
mean free path: the mean distance a molecule moves between two collisions (m)
\(\ell\)
the smallest length in the flow, for example a gap or a thin layer (m)

Read it aloudThe Knudsen number is the molecular step length divided by the smallest flow length. When it is much less than one, the fluid is a continuum.

Pope (2000) Eq. 2.1, p. 11

Technical notes & course context Go further

A drop of water holds a very large number of molecules. Each molecule moves in a random direction. CFD does not follow them. It uses averages: the mass in a small volume divided by that volume gives the density. The mean velocity of the molecules in that volume gives the velocity.

The averaging volume must have a correct size. If it is too small, it holds only a few molecules, and the average jumps about. If it is too large, it mixes regions where the flow is really different. Between these two limits, the average does not change with the size. That middle range is why the continuum idea works.

The ratio that tells you if the continuum idea holds is the Knudsen number. It compares the distance a molecule moves between two collisions with the smallest size in the flow.

Why this matters for your thesis

In your water flow the smallest flow lengths are far larger than the molecular lengths, so Kn ≪ 1 and the continuum holds. The fluid then has a smooth velocity, pressure and temperature field, and the equations in module A2 apply. Each OpenFOAM cell holds one value of each: an average of that smooth field over the cell. A cell size is a numerical choice; it does not decide whether the continuum holds.

02
FLUID BEHAVIOR

Stand still. Then ride along.

A field is a map: velocity, pressure, or temperature at every location. A particle is a small piece of fluid moving through that map.

PREDICT FIRSTWater enters a narrowing passage steadily. Can a particle accelerate?
The reasoning

Yes. It travels into regions with larger velocity. Steady means no change at a fixed location; it does not mean no acceleration.

Fixed probe · Eulerian

Ask: what passes this location as time goes by?

Moving particle · Lagrangian

Ask: what happens to this piece of fluid along its journey?

Steady flow

The map stays the same. A moving particle can still speed up, slow down, or turn.

ENGINEER’S HABIT

When you inspect a steady CFD result, mentally follow a particle through the velocity field.

Technical notes & course context Go further

A fluid particle is the smallest piece of fluid that still has a continuum value. It moves with the local flow. There are two ways to describe a flow:

  • Eulerian: stand at fixed points and record what passes. Each point has a value at each time. This is a field.
  • Lagrangian: follow one particle and record what happens to it.

The two views give different stories in the same flow. In the figure the flow is steady, so the colour of each cell never changes. But the particle slows down in front of the cylinder and speeds up over its top.

In OpenFOAM

The file 0/U holds one velocity per cell: an Eulerian field. A streamline or particle track in ParaView is Lagrangian: ParaView rides the field. The equations CFD solves are written for fixed cells, but they come from laws about particles. Module A2 links the two.

03
FLUID BEHAVIOR

Density stores mass. Pressure pushes.

Density tells you how much mass fits into a volume. Pressure tells you the normal force per area on a surface.

PREDICT FIRSTIf water is modeled as incompressible, must its pressure be constant?
The reasoning

No. Density can be constant while pressure varies. That pressure field helps direct the flow and enforce mass conservation.

Density · ρ · kg/m³

More mass in the same volume means greater density.

Pressure · p · Pa

Pressure acts on every face. A pressure difference creates a net push.

Incompressible model

A fluid parcel keeps its density. Pressure can still change greatly.

ENGINEER’S HABIT

A pressure contour is useful when you ask what its differences do to the fluid.

Technical notes & course context Go further

Density \(\rho\) is mass per unit volume, in kg/m³. Your thesis water at 24.0 °C has \(\rho = 997.3\) kg/m³ (proposal §5.4).

A gas gets denser when you push on it. A liquid almost does not. Versteeg and Malalasekera treat liquids, and gases at low speed, as incompressible: density does not change (1995, pp. 13, 21). Your thesis flow is incompressible.

Pressure \(p\) is the normal force per unit area that the fluid puts on a surface, in Pa (N/m²). In a gas, pressure links to density and temperature through an equation of state. In an incompressible flow there is no such link (Pope 2000, p. 18). Pressure then has a different job: it takes the value that keeps mass in balance everywhere. Module A2 shows this job.

In OpenFOAM

The incompressibleFluid solver stores kinematic pressure: pressure divided by density, \(p/\rho\), in m²/s². The OpenFOAM-14 tutorial pitzDaily declares 0/p with dimensions [kinematicPressure]. So 1 bar (10⁵ Pa) in your water is 10⁵ / 997.3 ≈ 100.3 m²/s² in the file.

04
FLUID BEHAVIOR

Viscosity couples neighboring layers

Move the upper plate. The fluid touching it moves with it. The stationary lower plate holds its touching fluid still.

PREDICT FIRSTKeep the plate speed and gap fixed. If viscosity doubles, what happens to the shear stress?
The reasoning

It doubles for this Newtonian, fully developed plate flow. The imposed velocity gradient stays the same; the stress increases.

No-slip

At an ordinary solid wall, fluid velocity matches wall velocity.

Velocity gradient

Layers between the walls move at different speeds. A sharper change means a steeper gradient.

Shear stress

Viscosity transfers momentum between the layers. At fixed gradient, larger viscosity means larger shear stress.

The relationship

Same gap + twice the plate speed → twice the velocity gradient → twice the shear stress.

ENGINEER’S HABIT

Wall friction depends on the near-wall velocity gradient. Average speed alone cannot tell you the wall stress.

The equation, in plain language Optional depth
\[ \tau = \mu\,\frac{du}{dy} \]
\(\tau\)
shear stress: sliding force per unit area between layers (Pa)
\(\mu\)
dynamic viscosity (Pa·s)
\(du/dy\)
how fast the velocity changes across the layers (1/s)

Read it aloudThe shear stress is the viscosity times the rate at which velocity changes from layer to layer. Steeper change, more friction.

Newton's law of viscosity: the one-direction case of Versteeg & Malalasekera (1995) Eq. 2.31, p. 22 (\(v = 0\))

\[ \nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho} \]
\(\nu\)
kinematic viscosity (m²/s)
\(\mu\)
dynamic viscosity (Pa·s)
\(\rho\)
density (kg/m³)

Read it aloudKinematic viscosity, "nu", is dynamic viscosity per unit density. Its unit is m²/s. Section 05 shows why: it measures how fast momentum spreads.

Pope (2000) p. 17

Technical notes & course context Go further

Put water between two plates and move the top plate. The water touching each plate moves with that plate. This is the no-slip condition. Pope writes it for a wall at rest: no flow through the wall and no sliding along it, so the fluid velocity there is zero (2000, Eqs. 2.36–2.37). For a moving wall the same rule says the fluid velocity equals the wall velocity. Between the plates, the water slides in layers. Each layer drags its neighbours. The friction between layers is a shear stress, and the fluid property that sets it is the dynamic viscosity \(\mu\).

CFD equations usually use the kinematic viscosity: dynamic viscosity divided by density.

Warm water is less viscous. From 10 °C to 80 °C, \(\nu\) falls to less than a third. Section 06 shows the full curve.

05
FLUID BEHAVIOR

Heat can travel without the fluid

A moving fluid carries thermal energy with it. Even in still fluid, conduction spreads heat from hot regions toward cold ones.

PREDICT FIRSTIn the wall experiment below, Pr is greater than one. Does heat spread farther than momentum?
The reasoning

No. Here momentum diffuses faster. In this diffusion-only experiment, penetration distances scale with the square root of diffusivity, so their ratio is √Pr.

Conductivity · k

Sets heat flux for a given temperature gradient.

Specific heat · c

Sets the energy needed to warm a kilogram by one kelvin.

Diffusivity · α

Combines conduction and thermal storage. It controls how quickly temperature differences spread.

Prandtl number · Pr

Momentum diffusivity ÷ thermal diffusivity. Above one: momentum diffuses faster. Below one: heat diffuses faster.

ENGINEER’S HABIT

A mesh that captures velocity can still miss a sharper temperature variation near a heated surface.

The equation, in plain language Optional depth
\[ \mathbf{q} = -k\,\nabla T \]
\(\mathbf{q}\)
heat flux: heat flow per unit area (W/m²)
\(k\)
thermal conductivity (W/m·K)
\(\nabla T\)
temperature gradient: how fast temperature changes with distance, and in which direction (K/m)

Read it aloudHeat flows from hot to cold (the minus sign), and the flow is the conductivity times the temperature gradient.

Versteeg & Malalasekera (1995) Eq. 2.20, p. 19

\[ \mathrm{Pr} = \frac{\nu}{\alpha} \]
\(\mathrm{Pr}\)
Prandtl number (dimensionless)
\(\nu\)
momentum diffusivity (m²/s)
\(\alpha\)
thermal diffusivity (m²/s)

Read it aloudThe Prandtl number is how fast momentum spreads divided by how fast heat spreads. Above one, momentum wins.

Pope (2000) §2.6, p. 21

Technical notes & course context Go further

Two more properties control heat. Thermal conductivity \(k\) sets how much heat flows through the fluid for a given temperature change over distance. This is Fourier's law:

Specific heat \(c\) is the energy needed to make 1 kg of fluid 1 K warmer (J/kg·K). Conductivity moves heat; specific heat stores it. Their ratio, per unit density, is the thermal diffusivity \(\alpha = k/(\rho c)\), in m²/s. With constant \(k\), it is the number in front of the conduction term when the temperature equation is divided by \(\rho c\) (Versteeg & Malalasekera 1995, Eq. 2.25).

Now \(\nu\) and \(\alpha\) have the same unit, m²/s. Each one is a diffusivity: \(\nu\) spreads momentum, \(\alpha\) spreads heat. Their ratio is the Prandtl number:

Why this matters for your thesis

At 24 °C, the existing proposal uses Pr = 6.30 (§5.4). The diffusion-only model above has a thinner thermal penetration region than momentum penetration region. In a real turbulent rod bundle, thermal resolution also depends on turbulent transport and wall treatment. Check both velocity and temperature resolution.

06
FLUID BEHAVIOR

“Water” is not one property set

Temperature can change viscosity far more than density. A constant-property calculation deliberately freezes those changes.

PREDICT FIRSTAt the same flow speed, are cold-water and warm-water CFD cases physically identical?
The reasoning

No. Their viscosities differ, changing the balance between inertia and viscous effects. Thermal properties also change.

Compare the curves

Each plot has its own scale. Read the values rather than comparing line heights.

Watch viscosity

At the same speed and geometry, lower kinematic viscosity means a higher Reynolds number.

Check the assumption

Choose properties for the actual operating condition. Revisit constant properties when temperatures vary substantially.

ENGINEER’S HABIT

Write down the fluid state alongside every property value: temperature, pressure, and the model used.

Technical notes & course context Go further

Move the slider and watch the four properties change. From 5 to 95 °C, density changes by about 4% and conductivity by about 19%. Viscosity changes by a factor of about 5, and the Prandtl number by about 6. In CFD, viscosity is the property that matters most for flow, because it sets the Reynolds number (module A3).

The reactor is different

The first proposal used reactor conditions: 552 K and 15.52 MPa (~/Projects/msc-research/raw/RESEARCH_STATUS.md). At that state, IAPWS-IF97 gives \(\rho\) = 766.3 kg/m³, \(\nu\) = 1.265 × 10⁻⁷ m²/s and Pr = 0.82. So reactor water spreads heat faster than momentum, which is the opposite of your 24 °C validation water. Your current proposal uses 24 °C because Byun's measurements were made at 24 °C.

07
FLUID BEHAVIOR

Translate the physics into OpenFOAM

Before copying a value into a file, ask which quantity the solver expects and which units it uses.

PREDICT FIRSTAn incompressibleFluid result has a pressure difference of 50 m²/s². Is that 50 Pa?
The reasoning

No. Multiply by density. With the existing course’s 24 °C water value, 50 × 997.3 = 49,865 Pa. Always check field dimensions and pressure references.

Velocity

A vector: three components, in m/s.

Kinematic viscosity

ν = μ/ρ, in m²/s. Do not paste dynamic viscosity into a field expecting ν.

Kinematic pressure

In the incompressibleFluid example, p represents pressure divided by density, in m²/s².

ENGINEER’S HABIT

Units are a practical debugging tool. Check them before diagnosing the numerical method.

DM-08

Check yourself

A finer mesh gives a smoother temperature contour. Has that established that the continuum assumption is valid?

No. Mesh resolution and continuum validity are different checks. Continuum validity depends on molecular versus physical flow scales; numerical accuracy needs its own verification.

A steady flow turns around a bend at constant speed. Is its acceleration zero?

No. Velocity includes direction. A particle accelerates when its direction changes, even if its speed stays constant.

Two adjacent fluid layers move at the same velocity. Must a large viscosity create a large shear stress between them?

No. In the simple shear model, the velocity gradient is zero, so that shear stress is zero. Viscosity is a property; stress also depends on deformation.

Two fluids have the same conductivity and density, but one has twice the specific heat. Which has the lower thermal diffusivity?

The one with twice the specific heat. Its thermal diffusivity is half as large: the same conduction must warm a greater thermal storage capacity.

You use the same value of dynamic viscosity in a kinematic-viscosity field. What check catches this before a run?

The units: dynamic viscosity is in Pa·s, whereas kinematic viscosity is in m²/s. Divide dynamic viscosity by density to obtain the required value.

Words to keep

Open the chapter glossary
continuum
A model of a fluid as a smooth material with a value at each point, instead of single molecules.
fluid particle
The smallest piece of fluid that has a stable continuum value. It moves with the flow.
mean free path (λ)
The mean distance a molecule moves between two collisions.
Knudsen number (Kn)
Mean free path divided by the smallest flow length. Much less than one: continuum.
field
A value at each point in space, at each time. Velocity and temperature are fields.
Eulerian
Describing the flow at fixed points. CFD cells are Eulerian.
Lagrangian
Describing the flow by following particles.
density (ρ)
Mass per unit volume, kg/m³.
incompressible
Density is constant. A good model for liquids and for gases at low speed. Your thesis also holds density constant with temperature.
pressure (p)
Normal force per unit area, Pa.
kinematic pressure
Pressure divided by density, m²/s². What incompressibleFluid stores.
no-slip condition
Fluid touching a wall moves with the wall.
shear stress (τ)
Sliding force per unit area between fluid layers, Pa.
dynamic viscosity (μ)
The property that sets shear stress for a given velocity gradient, Pa·s.
kinematic viscosity (ν)
μ/ρ, m²/s. How fast momentum spreads.
thermal conductivity (k)
How well the fluid conducts heat, W/m·K.
specific heat (c)
Energy to make 1 kg 1 K warmer, J/kg·K.
thermal diffusivity (α)
k/(ρc), m²/s. How fast heat spreads.
Prandtl number (Pr)
ν/α. How fast momentum spreads compared with heat.
